Tipping in the US can feel confusing, especially now that a tip screen shows up almost everywhere. Here are the common ranges, where tips really matter, and an easy way to do the math in your head.

Why tips matter so much in the US

In many states, restaurants can pay servers a lower "tipped minimum wage" and count tips toward the regular minimum wage. Rules vary a lot by state, but in practice, tips are a large part of what servers, bartenders and delivery drivers earn. That is why sit-down restaurant tips are still expected, even when other tip requests feel optional.

These are common customs, not rules. Tip more for great service, a complicated order or a long stay at a busy table.

Should you tip on the total before or after tax?

Traditionally, the tip is calculated on the pre-tax amount. Many people simply tip on the total because it is easier, and the difference is small. On a $60 meal with 8% tax, 20% before tax is $12.00 and 20% after tax is $12.96.

Quick mental math

  • 10%: move the decimal point one place left. $46.80 becomes $4.68.
  • 20%: double the 10%. $4.68 × 2 = $9.36, so about $9.50.
  • 15%: 10% plus half of it. $4.68 + $2.34 = $7.02.
  • 18%: 20% minus 2% (2% is 10% divided by five). $9.36 − $0.94 = $8.42.

What about the tip screen?

Tip screens often start at 18%, 20% or 25%, sometimes on things like a bottle of water. You can choose "custom" or "no tip" for counter service where there was little service. Tip where someone served you at your table, brought food to your door or did a personal service like a haircut.
