"40% off, plus an extra 20% at the register" sounds like 60% off. It is not. Here is how to work out what you will really pay, including sales tax, in a few seconds.

The basic discount

Sale price = original price × (1 − discount ÷ 100)

A $80 jacket at 25% off: 80 × 0.75 = $60. You save $20.

Quick trick: to take 25% off, find what you pay (75%), not what you save. For 30% off pay 70%, for 40% off pay 60%.

Stacked discounts do not add up

When a store offers "40% off, then an extra 20% off", the second discount applies to the already reduced price:

  • $100 × 0.60 = $60 after the first discount.
  • $60 × 0.80 = $48 after the extra 20%.
  • Total saving: $52, which is 52% off, not 60%.

The order does not matter: 20% then 40% gives the same $48.

Sales tax

Total = price × (1 + tax rate ÷ 100)

A $48 purchase with 7.25% sales tax: 48 × 1.0725 = $51.48.

In most states, sales tax is calculated on the price after the store's discount. Rates are set by the state plus the county and city, so the same item can cost a different amount in two nearby towns. Four states have no statewide sales tax: Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon. Alaska has no state sales tax but many local ones. Some states also treat groceries or clothing differently, so check your local rate.

Coupons: dollars off vs. percent off

A "$20 off $100" coupon is 20% on a $100 order, but only 10% if you spend $200. If you can use either "$20 off" or "15% off", the percent is better once your order is above $133.33 (because 15% of $133.33 is $20).

How much did you really save?

Percent saved = (original − paid) ÷ original × 100

Paid $48 for something that was $100: (100 − 48) ÷ 100 = 52% saved.

Is it really a deal?

  • Compare the sale price to what the item usually sells for, not to the "list price".
  • Add shipping and tax before comparing two stores.
  • Set a budget first. Buying something you did not plan to buy is not a saving.
