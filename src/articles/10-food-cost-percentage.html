Food cost percentage tells you how much of each dollar on the menu goes to ingredients. It is one of the most useful numbers in a restaurant, and it only takes a few minutes to calculate.

For a single dish

Food cost % = cost of ingredients ÷ menu price × 100

A burger that costs $3.90 in ingredients and sells for $13: 3.90 ÷ 13 × 100 = 30%.

To get the ingredient cost, list every item on the plate with the amount used and its price per unit: the bun, the patty, cheese, sauce, garnish, the side, even the oil for frying. Small things add up.

For the whole restaurant (a month)

Food cost % = (beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory) ÷ food sales × 100

Example: you start the month with $8,000 of inventory, buy $22,000, and end with $7,000. You used $23,000 of food. With $72,000 in food sales, the food cost is 23,000 ÷ 72,000 = about 32%.

This number includes waste, spills, staff meals and over-portioning, so it is usually higher than the average of your recipe costs. The gap between the two shows how much is being lost.

What is a good food cost?

Many restaurants aim for roughly 28% to 35%, but it depends on the concept. Pizza and pasta places often run lower, while steakhouses and seafood restaurants often run higher. What matters most is your total "prime cost" (food plus labor), which many operators try to keep around 60% to 65% of sales or less.

Setting a menu price from cost

Menu price = ingredient cost ÷ target food cost %

A pasta dish costs $3.20 to make and your target is 28%: 3.20 ÷ 0.28 = $11.43, so you might price it at $11.50 or $11.95. Then check it against your competitors and what your guests expect. Some items (drinks, pasta, fries) can carry a low food cost, which makes room for a few popular items with a higher one.

Ways to lower food cost

  • Standard recipes and portions: use scales and portion scoops so every plate is the same.
  • Track waste: a simple waste sheet in the kitchen shows what is being thrown away.
  • First in, first out: date everything and use older stock first.
  • Cross-use ingredients across several dishes so less goes bad.
  • Review prices often: when a supplier raises prices, recalculate the dishes that use that item.
  • Count inventory on the same day each week or month, so the numbers are comparable.
