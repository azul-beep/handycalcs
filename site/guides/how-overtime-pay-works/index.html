How overtime pay is calculated

Worked a long week? If you are an hourly employee, those extra hours may be worth 50% more. Here is how overtime is usually calculated in the US and how to check your paycheck.

The federal rule: over 40 hours a week

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees must be paid at least 1.5 times their regular rate for every hour worked over 40 in a workweek. That is "time and a half".

  • The rule is per workweek, a fixed 7-day period set by the employer. Hours are not averaged across two weeks.
  • Federal law does not require overtime for working more than 8 hours in a day, on weekends or on holidays (though some employers and some states do pay it).
  • Paid time off, like vacation or holiday pay, usually does not count as hours worked for the 40-hour total.

Example

You earn $20 an hour and work 46 hours in one week.

  • Regular pay: 40 × $20 = $800
  • Overtime rate: $20 × 1.5 = $30 an hour
  • Overtime pay: 6 × $30 = $180
  • Total for the week: $980 (before taxes)

State rules can be better

States can require more than the federal minimum, and some do. California is the best-known example: overtime after 8 hours in a day, double time after 12 hours in a day, and special rules for the seventh day in a row. A few other states have daily overtime rules too. When state and federal law differ, the rule that is better for the worker applies, so check your state's labor department website.

Who does not get overtime?

"Exempt" employees are not covered by overtime rules. To be exempt, a job usually has to pay a salary above a minimum level and involve certain executive, administrative or professional duties. A job title alone does not make someone exempt, and being paid a salary does not automatically mean no overtime. If you are unsure, the US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division has free information.

Tips, bonuses and the "regular rate"

For tipped workers, overtime is based on the full minimum wage, not just the cash wage. Some bonuses (for example, production or attendance bonuses) must be included in the regular rate used for overtime, which can raise the overtime pay a little. Payroll should handle this, but it is good to know when you check a paycheck.
