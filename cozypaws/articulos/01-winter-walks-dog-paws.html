Most dogs love snow. Their paws are a different story. Road salt, ice melt and packed snow between the toes cause most winter paw problems, and nearly all of them are easy to prevent with a few habits.

1. Wipe paws as soon as you get home

Sidewalk salt and many ice melts dry out and crack paw pads, and they sting in small cuts. Dogs also lick their feet clean, so whatever is on the paw ends up in the mouth. Keep an old towel and a bowl of lukewarm water by the door and wipe each paw, including between the toes, after every winter walk. Pet wipes work too.

2. Trim the fur between the toes

Long-haired dogs collect little ice balls between their pads. They pull on the skin and can make a dog limp or stop walking. Keeping that fur trimmed level with the pads (a groomer can do it in minutes) stops most snowballs from forming.

3. Use paw balm or booties

A thin layer of paw balm or wax before the walk acts as a barrier against salt and ice. If your dog will tolerate them, booties are even better, especially in cities that use a lot of de-icer. Give your dog a few short practice sessions indoors with treats before the first real walk: almost every dog walks funny in booties at first.

4. Watch out for antifreeze

Antifreeze made with ethylene glycol tastes sweet and is extremely toxic to dogs and cats, even in small amounts. Keep walks away from puddles in driveways and parking lots, clean up any drips in your garage and choose a pet-safer product (propylene glycol based) if you top up your own car. If you think your pet licked antifreeze, call your vet or an emergency clinic right away: treatment works best in the first hours.

5. Adjust the length of the walk

How much cold a dog can handle depends on size, coat, age and health. A Husky and a Chihuahua live in very different winters. As a general guide:

  • Small, short-haired, very young and senior dogs get cold fastest. Several short walks beat one long one.
  • A sweater or coat helps dogs with thin coats, like Greyhounds, Whippets and Frenchies.
  • Signs your dog has had enough: lifting paws, shivering, whining, slowing down or trying to turn back home.
  • Wind and wet snow make it feel much colder than the thermometer says.

6. Check the paws once a week

Look for cracks, redness, cuts or swelling between the toes. Small dry spots usually improve with balm. Bleeding cracks, limping or a dog that keeps licking one paw are worth a call to your vet.
