You have probably heard that one dog year equals seven human years. It is an easy rule, but it is not how dogs age. Dogs grow up very fast in their first two years, then age more slowly, and big dogs age faster than small ones.

Why the 7-year rule does not work

A one year old dog is not like a seven year old child. Most dogs can have puppies around their first birthday and have their adult size by then, which is much closer to a human teenager. After that, aging slows down. That is why vets and veterinary groups use a curve instead of a single number.

A more realistic way to count

  • First year: about 15 human years.
  • Second year: about 9 more, so a 2 year old dog is about 24.
  • After that: each year adds about 4 to 7 human years, depending on size. Small dogs add about 4, medium dogs 5, large dogs 6 and giant breeds about 7.

These are estimates. Genes, weight, exercise and care all change how fast a dog ages, so two dogs of the same age and size can be quite different.

What about the "DNA" formula?

In 2019, researchers who studied changes in DNA in Labrador Retrievers proposed a formula: human age = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31. It also shows very fast aging early on (a 1 year old Lab comes out at about 31) and slower aging later. It was based on one breed, so it is interesting but not the final word for every dog.

Why it matters

Knowing your dog's "human" age helps you think about care. Large breeds may be seniors by age 6 or 7, small breeds closer to 9 or 10. Many vets suggest checkups twice a year for senior dogs, plus attention to joints, teeth and weight.
